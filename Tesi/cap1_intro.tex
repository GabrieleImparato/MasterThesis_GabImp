In the field of industrial warehouse storage, one of the main challenges is the full automation of the processes carried out within a facility, ranging from mission scheduling to the determination of the optimal routes to be followed inside the warehouse. When a warehouse is designed and built from scratch, introducing automated systems is generally easier, since the layout and infrastructure can be specifically planned to accommodate them. Conversely, automating an existing facility is considerably more challenging, particularly when Automated Guided Vehicles (AGVs) need to operate in environments that were originally designed for manually operated forklifts and human workers. In such cases, one possible approach is to redesign the operational workflow and use simulation and optimization algorithms to determine how available resources, such as AGVs, forklifts, and human operators, should be allocated and coordinated.

The algorithms adopted are not necessarily the same for every application, since each use case generally presents different constraints and requirements. Therefore, it is often neither convenient nor effective to directly reuse an existing solution without adapting it to the specific problem. Instead, new solutions may need to be developed by building on well-established algorithms and heuristics.

For example, the layout of a warehouse can be represented as a grid and, more generally, abstracted as a graph composed of nodes and edges. In this representation, nodes may correspond to relevant locations or positions within the environment, while edges describe the possible connections or movements between them. One of the most widely used algorithms for finding a path through a graph is A*. By combining the actual cost of reaching a node with a heuristic estimate of the remaining cost to the destination, A* can efficiently determine an optimal path under appropriate conditions~\cite{hart1968formal}. However, in practical applications, the standard algorithm may need to be adapted or extended in order to account for the specific characteristics and constraints of the problem under consideration.

Another important aspect to consider is how each mission is modeled. In particular, material-handling operations can often be summarized as \textit{loading} and \textit{unloading} tasks. Each of these operations can in turn be represented through a sequence of discrete phases, such as \textbf{start}, \textbf{execution}, and \textbf{completion}.
\[
\text{Start} \rightarrow \text{Execution} \rightarrow \text{Completion}
\]
This abstraction is useful because it allows a continuous real-world operation to be represented as a sequence of discrete states or events. In this way, an algorithm can reason about the current status of each mission, assign the required resources, coordinate multiple concurrent tasks, and schedule their execution while avoiding conflicts and, when properly designed, deadlock situations.
In automation and discrete-event systems, several modeling approaches can be used to represent this type of behavior. Two common examples are \textit{finite-state machines} (FSMs) and \textit{Petri nets}. Both approaches can also be extended with timing information or time constraints in order to represent and simulate real-world processes more accurately.

In particular, \textit{Petri nets} are particularly well suited to this type of problem when multiple missions must be executed concurrently while sharing a limited set of resources. Compared with a representation based exclusively on FSMs, Petri nets can provide a more natural and compact representation of concurrency, synchronization, resource availability, mutual exclusion, and conflicts between different activities. For example, several missions may simultaneously require the same combination of resources, such as a forklift and an operator. A Petri-net model can explicitly represent the availability of these resources and ensure that a mission can proceed only when all the required resources are available. This representation is also useful for analyzing resource-allocation conflicts and identifying or preventing possible deadlock conditions.

In other cases, it may be useful to schedule the missions in advance, for example by planning the activities for the following working day. This makes it possible to determine beforehand how the available resources will be allocated, identify potential scheduling conflicts, and define suitable strategies to handle them before operations begin. Advance scheduling can also be used to improve workforce planning. For instance, the workload can be distributed among operators so that the duration of each shift remains as close as possible to the target working time, such as eight hours, while minimizing overtime. At the same time, the schedule can be optimized to reduce operating costs associated with the use of material-handling resources, including forklift energy consumption, fuel consumption, and unnecessary movements.

Another relevant constraint is related to outbound logistics. Trucks often need to be loaded before specific departure times defined by the shipping schedule. Consequently, the missions associated with the goods assigned to a particular truck must be completed before its loading deadline. This introduces a scheduling problem with both resource constraints and time constraints. Optimization techniques such as \textit{linear programming} or, more commonly when discrete assignment decisions are involved, \textit{mixed-integer linear programming} (MILP) can be used to determine a feasible and efficient schedule. A possible approach is to identify, among the complete set of warehouse missions, those associated with a specific truck and assign them a higher scheduling priority according to the vehicle's departure deadline. These missions can then be optimized separately or incorporated into the global scheduling model with appropriate time constraints and priority rules. In this way, the scheduler can ensure that time-critical loading operations are completed while still considering the allocation of shared resources and the remaining warehouse activities.

Speaking about advance planning, another possible approach comes from the field of predictive control, namely \textit{Model Predictive Control} (MPC). MPC uses a mathematical model of the system to optimize a sequence of future decisions over a finite \textit{prediction horizon}. In the context of warehouse scheduling, the decision variables may represent, for example, the allocation of available resources, the assignment of missions, or, depending on the adopted model, routing decisions. The prediction horizon may correspond to several hours or even to a longer planning interval. One of the main advantages of MPC is its receding-horizon strategy. Instead of computing a complete plan once and keeping it unchanged, MPC repeatedly solves the optimization problem using the most recent information available. Only the first part of the computed solution is applied, after which the optimization is performed again. This makes the approach particularly interesting in environments where delays, resource unavailability, new missions, or other unexpected events may occur.

Although MPC has traditionally been associated with the control of dynamical systems, it can also be extended to discrete, hybrid, and discrete-event systems. Therefore, its application to warehouse scheduling requires an appropriate model capable of representing discrete decisions such as mission assignment, resource allocation, and task sequencing. For longer-term planning, such as the organization of several working days, an MPC-based strategy could also be considered. However, increasing the prediction horizon generally \textit{increases the computational complexity of the optimization problem}. For this reason, a hierarchical approach may be preferable, in which long-term planning determines the overall allocation of missions and resources, while shorter-horizon optimization is used to react to changes during daily operations.

\section{Scope and contribution of this work}

The solution developed in this thesis uses a heuristic scheduler coupled
with a Colored Timed Petri Net and A* path planning. Orders are known at
the beginning of the simulation, while resource assignments are made
progressively. Each order receives a single owner when its first mission
is queued and retains that owner even if execution spans multiple batches.

Global batches have a target number of successful mission completions.
Short resource queues are replenished during execution, respecting
compatibility, internal priorities and the places remaining in the target.
Load balancing combines completed processing time and the estimated duration
of queued work. The Petri-net marking persists between batches, preserving
the state of unfinished orders.

The common batch deadline and the cumulative order deadlines provide
performance references. Exceeding them does not interrupt missions or
prevent replenishment required to reach the target. The experiments examine
workload distribution, resource utilization and completion punctuality.
The MILP and MPC approaches discussed above provide broader context and
possible extensions; the implemented policy does not solve those optimization
problems. Likewise, routing uses a static map without modeling collisions
between moving resources or optimizing energy consumption.

Chapter~2 introduces the event, Petri-net and scheduling concepts used by
the model. Chapter~3 develops the queue policy, the persistent execution
state, geometric estimates and the experimental comparison of batch targets.
